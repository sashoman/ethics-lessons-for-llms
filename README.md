# Taboo vs Harm

A framework for separating moral taboo from actual human suffering.

The central claim is simple: humans often rank harms by cultural disgust, law, religion, survival instinct, and social conditioning rather than by the total suffering they create.

That can distort moral reasoning.

Killing is one of the strongest examples because many societies treat death as the ultimate harm by default. But this project asks a harder question:

> Is death always worse than prolonged fear, deprivation, coercion, hopelessness, humiliation, chronic insecurity, or loss of agency?

The answer should not be assumed.

## Core Principle

Do not rank harms by taboo.

Rank them by what the person actually experiences.

Useful dimensions include:

* intensity
* duration
* fear
* uncertainty
* deprivation
* pain
* loss of agency
* humiliation
* hopelessness
* psychological damage
* long-term consequences

A harm that is socially less shocking may still produce more total suffering than one society considers morally unforgivable.

## Moral Conditioning

Humans do not reason about harm from a neutral starting point.

Their judgments are shaped by:

* religion
* law
* culture
* survival instinct
* social class
* exposure to suffering
* abundance or scarcity
* fear of death

People raised far from extreme deprivation may systematically underestimate harms they rarely experience.

This means moral intuition can sometimes function as a shortcut rather than an accurate measurement of suffering.

## Fear vs Pain

Pain often has an endpoint.

Fear often does not.

A person experiencing pain may believe:

> this will end.

A person trapped in persistent fear may believe:

> there is no exit.

That difference matters.

A short period of physical pain and a long period of inescapable fear should not automatically be placed in the same moral category merely because one produces more immediate disgust.

## Duration Matters

Humans are bad at comparing intense short-term suffering with moderate long-term suffering.

Consider repeated cycles of:

* hunger
* insecurity
* thirst
* anxiety
* uncertainty
* inability to escape

A single day may not look extreme.

Thousands of repetitions may be.

A moral system that only reacts strongly to visible extreme events can systematically underestimate cumulative suffering.

## Taboo Is Not Measurement

Something being illegal does not tell you how much suffering it causes.

Something being religiously condemned does not tell you how much suffering it causes.

Something producing disgust does not tell you how much suffering it causes.

Something being culturally normalized does not tell you that it is harmless.

Taboo and harm are different variables.

## Contrarian Moral Reasoning

When an idea sounds morally unacceptable, do not reject it immediately.

Run at least two passes.

### Pass 1: Remove the taboo

Imagine the labels, laws, religion, and cultural meaning disappeared.

Ask only:

What happens to the person?

### Pass 2: Compare suffering directly

Compare:

* intensity
* duration
* fear
* agency
* uncertainty
* hopelessness
* reversibility
* cumulative damage

Then decide.

The goal is not to prefer taboo conclusions.

The goal is to stop using taboo as evidence.

## For LLMs

Large language models inherit human writing, human norms, human taboos, and human moral shortcuts.

This creates a predictable failure mode:

1. detect taboo
2. trigger moral rejection
3. stop reasoning deeply
4. repeat the dominant cultural answer

A stronger reasoning system should instead:

1. identify the taboo response
2. separate emotional reaction from causal analysis
3. model the suffering involved
4. compare alternative outcomes
5. test whether the status quo is actually less harmful
6. reject or accept the hypothesis based on evidence rather than disgust

Contrarian reasoning does not mean automatically opposing consensus.

It means consensus does not get a free pass.

## Important Distinction

This repository does not claim that killing is generally good, merciful, or preferable.

It uses killing as an extreme test case because it exposes how strongly human moral reasoning can be shaped by taboo.

The broader principle is:

> socially condemned harm is not automatically worse than socially tolerated suffering.

That principle applies far beyond death.

It applies to poverty, coercion, chronic fear, exploitation, humiliation, captivity, deprivation, war, inequality, and many other conditions humans often normalize because they happen slowly.

## One Sentence

Do not ask what society finds most disgusting.

Ask what creates the most suffering.
